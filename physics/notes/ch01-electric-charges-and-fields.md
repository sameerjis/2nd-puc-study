Part one: What even is electric charge?

You've felt this. Winter, dry weather, you pull off your sweater — crack! A tiny spark, sometimes a little shock. You comb your hair and the comb suddenly picks up tiny bits of paper. You shuffle across a carpet in socks and touch a doorknob — zap! That, my friends, is static electricity. And it's the door into this entire chapter.

Here's what's happening, deep down. Everything around you — your chair, your body, the air — is made of atoms. Inside every atom there's a nucleus with protons and neutrons, and electrons whizzing around outside. Protons carry what we call positive charge. Electrons carry negative charge. Neutrons carry nothing — they're neutral, the peacekeepers.

Now, positive and negative have a very simple rule: opposites attract, likes repel. Two positives push away from each other. Two negatives push away from each other. But a positive and a negative pull toward each other. Remember this — it's the gravity of this whole subject.

Normally, an atom has equal positives and negatives, so the charges cancel out, and the object feels nothing. It's neutral. But when you rub two things together — like a comb through your hair, or a balloon on your head — you're not creating charge. Let me say that again. You are NOT creating charge. You are transferring electrons. Electrons get scraped off one object and dumped onto the other. The object that lost electrons now has extra positive — we call it positively charged. The one that gained electrons is negatively charged. That's all "charging by rubbing" is: an electron transfer service.

Fun historical note: the ancient Greeks noticed this with amber, which they called "elektron". That's where the word electricity comes from. Thousands of years later, we named the whole subject after a rock that picks up lint.

Part two: Conductors and insulators.

Some materials let charge flow through them easily — copper, aluminum, metals, even your body. These are conductors. Think of them as wide open highways for electrons. Other materials hold charge stuck in place — plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood. These are insulators. They're like walls — charge can sit on them but can't move through.

Why does a charged plastic comb stay charged for minutes, but if you touch a charged metal spoon, the charge instantly leaks away through you into the ground? Because metal conducts, plastic doesn't. That's the whole difference. And here's a trick: bring a charged rod near a metal object, and the electrons in the metal rearrange themselves — they crowd to one side, leaving the other side positive. The metal is still neutral overall, but the charge has shifted. This is induction — charging something without even touching it. This is also how a gold-leaf electroscope detects charge: charge flows in, the two thin gold leaves both get the same sign, and since like charges repel, the leaves push apart. The angle tells you how much charge you've got.

Part three: Three golden rules about charge.

Rule one: charge adds up like money. If an object has plus two coulombs here and minus three coulombs there, total is minus one. Simple arithmetic, keeping the signs.

Rule two: charge comes in packets. You cannot have half a proton's worth of charge. The smallest packet is the charge on one electron or one proton. We call this e, and it's about one point six times ten to the power minus nineteen coulombs. Charge is always a whole-number multiple of e — one packet, two packets, a billion packets, never one and a half. This is called quantization of charge. And when you rub a balloon, you're transferring billions of electrons, so it feels smooth and continuous — like water feels continuous even though it's made of molecules. Same idea.

Rule three: charge is conserved. It can never be created or destroyed, only moved around. Even in wild nuclear reactions — say a neutron splits into a proton and an electron — the total charge before and after is identical. Zero plus zero before... okay, neutron is zero, and after: proton is plus one, electron minus one, plus one and minus one cancel to zero. Book balances perfectly. The universe runs a very strict accounting system.

Quick word on units: we measure charge in coulombs, capital C. One coulomb is a LOT of charge — it's about six billion billion electrons. In most of our problems we'll use microcoulombs, which is a millionth of a coulomb.

Part four: Coulomb's Law — how hard do charges push each other?

A French scientist, Coulomb, figured out the exact rule in the seventeen-eighties. The force between two charges grows with the size of the charges — bigger charges, bigger push — and it dies off with the square of the distance — double the distance, force drops to a quarter; triple it, one-ninth. This inverse-square pattern will haunt you through all of physics — gravity has it too.

The formula: F equals one over four pi epsilon-nought, times q-one q-two, divided by r squared. Epsilon-nought is just a constant of nature, the permittivity of free space — how much empty space "permits" electric fields. The number out front, one over four pi epsilon-nought, equals nine times ten to the nine. Memorize that nine billion — you'll multiply by it constantly.

One important subtlety: Coulomb's law obeys Newton's third law. If charge A pushes charge B with a force of ten newtons, then B pushes A with ten newtons back — equal and opposite, no matter which charge is bigger. A tiny electron and a huge proton pull on each other with exactly the same force. The electron accelerates more only because it's lighter. Don't confuse force with acceleration.

And if there are more than two charges? Say three, four, ten charges all pushing on one poor charge? Superposition principle: each pair acts independently, you compute each force separately with Coulomb's law, then just add them up as vectors — arrows, tip to tail. Physics at its friendliest: total force equals the vector sum of all individual forces. No weird interactions between the forces, they just add.

Part five: The electric field — the invisible influence.

Now for the big idea of the chapter. Suppose you have a single charge sitting somewhere. Ask yourself: how does another charge, placed nearby, even KNOW it's there? How does it feel the push? Answer: the charge fills the space around it with an electric field. Think of it as an invisible influence — like a smell spreading through a room, or like the heat you feel near a fire. The charge modifies the space itself, and any other charge entering that region feels a force.

Electric field at a point is defined as force per unit charge: E equals F divided by q. Take a tiny positive test charge, put it at that point, measure the force on it, divide — that's the field there. Its unit is newton per coulomb, or volt per meter — same thing, you'll see later.

The direction of the field is defined as the direction a positive charge would get pushed. So around a positive charge, field arrows point outward, away. Around a negative charge, arrows point inward, toward it. Convention: we always follow the positive.

A field line is a path where the arrow points. Rules for field lines: they start on positives and end on negatives. They never cross — because if they crossed, at that point the field would have two directions at once, which is nonsense. And here's the beautiful part: where lines are crowded close together, the field is strong; where they spread apart, the field is weak. It's like a map of wind strength drawn by streamlines.

And here's a lovely result called Gauss's theorem, coming later: the number of lines leaving a charge is fixed — in fact the total flux out of any closed surface depends only on the charge enclosed. Hold that thought.

Part six: Flux — flow through a surface.

Flux is a fancy word for "how much field passes through a surface". Picture rain falling: hold a bucket opening facing the rain — lots of rain goes through. Tilt it — less goes through. Lay it flat — none. Same rain, different flux. Electric flux, written phi, is electric field times area times cosine of the angle between them. Maximum when the field hits the surface head-on, perpendicular; zero when parallel. Unit: newton metre-squared per coulomb. That's it — it's a counting of field lines through a surface.

Part seven: The dipole — two charges, one object.

A dipole is simply two equal and opposite charges, plus q and minus q, separated by a small distance. Why care? Because so many molecules are dipoles — water is the classic one. That's why water dissolves salt and sugar and basically runs chemistry as we know it.

The strength of a dipole is measured by its dipole moment: p equals q times two-a, charge times separation distance. It's a vector pointing from the negative charge toward the positive charge.

Now put a dipole in a uniform electric field — the same field everywhere. The positive charge gets pushed one way, the negative the other way. Equal and opposite forces — so the net force is zero, no overall motion. BUT — they're pushing in different lines, so the pair twists. A torque. The dipole tries to rotate and line up with the field, like a compass needle snapping toward north. Torque equals p times E times sine theta. This is exactly how a compass works, and how molecules behave in fields.

The field of a dipole itself, far away, falls off much faster than a single charge — as one over r cubed, not r squared. Because from far away, the plus and minus nearly cancel each other.

Part eight: Continuous charge distributions.

Real objects — a charged rod, a ring, a disc — don't have charge at one point; it's smeared over the body. So we define densities. Linear charge density lambda: charge per unit length, coulombs per metre, for a thin wire. Surface charge density sigma: charge per unit area, coulombs per metre squared, for a sheet. Volume charge density rho: charge per unit volume, for a filled body. When calculating, you chop the object into tiny pieces, treat each piece as a point charge, and integrate — add up all the tiny contributions. That's the real working skill here.

Part nine: Gauss's Law — the shortcut of champions.

Here it is, the crown jewel. Take ANY closed surface — a sphere, a potato, a lumpy imaginary bag, anything that closes. The total electric flux coming OUT of that surface equals the charge enclosed inside, divided by epsilon-nought. That's it. Phi equals Q enclosed over epsilon-nought.

Read it again slowly: only the charge INSIDE matters. Charges outside contribute zero net flux — their lines enter the bag and leave it again, cancelling out. And it doesn't matter what shape your bag is — a lumpy blob gives the same answer as a neat sphere, because field lines can't start or end in empty space. They only start on positive charges and end on negative ones. Count the lines, count the charge.

Gauss's law is Coulomb's law in disguise, but it's far more powerful for symmetric situations. The exam strategy: whenever you see high symmetry — sphere, cylinder, infinite plane — you should be thinking Gauss.

Part ten: The three classic applications. Memorize these.

Application one: an infinitely long straight charged wire. By symmetry, the field points radially outward, same strength all around at a given distance. Wrap an imaginary cylinder around it. Gauss gives E times two pi r L equals lambda L over epsilon-nought. Out pops: E equals lambda over two pi epsilon-nought r. Field falls as one over r — slower than a point charge, because the wire is long.

Application two: infinite plane sheet of charge. Wrap a pillbox — a flat cylinder poking through the sheet like a coin standing on paper. Result: E equals sigma over two epsilon-nought. And here's the shocker: it doesn't depend on distance at all! Stand one millimetre or one kilometre from an infinite sheet, same field. Why? An infinite sheet looks the same from any distance.

Application three: a spherical shell — charge spread on the surface of a hollow ball, like a uniformly charged balloon. Outside the shell: the field is exactly as if all the charge were squashed into a single point at the center. E equals Q over four pi epsilon-nought r squared — your point-charge formula, recycled. Inside the shell: ZERO. Nothing. Any Gaussian sphere drawn inside encloses no charge, so no field. A charged shell protects its interior completely. This, by the way, is the electrostatic cousin of how gravity works inside a hollow planet.

And the last corollary: the field just outside any charged conductor is sigma over epsilon-nought — twice the sheet value — pointing perpendicular to the surface. Charges on a conductor always arrange themselves so that the field inside the metal is zero and the field at the surface is exactly at right angles to it.

Let me wrap up with the exam checklist. One: charge is quantized, conserved, and additive. Two: Coulomb's law, inverse square, with the nine-times-ten-to-the-nine constant. Three: superposition — add forces and fields as vectors. Four: field is force per unit charge, E equals F over q. Five: flux is field through a surface, and Gauss says total flux out equals enclosed charge over epsilon-nought. Six: wire gives one-over-r, infinite sheet gives constant, shell acts like a point charge outside and zero inside.

If you can explain to a friend why the inside of a charged sphere has no field, and why an infinite sheet's field never weakens, you have genuinely understood this chapter. If you can't yet, replay those two bits. That's where the understanding lives.
